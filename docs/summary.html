Summary

The Richmond region’s housing picture in one page: who is arriving, what exists, and what it costs. Each section links to the page with the figures and tables behind it. Trends compare the ACS 5-year windows ending 2014 and 2024 unless noted.

At a glance

The region is growing, and migration is doing the work

  • The region grew 10.7% to 1,341,248 people, led by Chesterfield (16.5%) and the four-locality expansion ring (15.5%); Richmond city grew 8.7% and Henrico 6.6%.
  • Natural increase fell from 4,228 people a year in 2010-2019 to 1,451 in 2020-2025, so net migration now supplies 90% of growth. In 10 of the 17 localities deaths exceeded births over 2020-2025.
  • International migration peaked at 10,787 in 2024; the latest year, 2025, was 5,387.
  • Richmond city flipped from domestic to international growth: its international migration rate rose from 2.8 to 6.8 per 1,000 a year while its domestic rate went from +5.3 to -1.1.

Full detail: Demographics

The population is older, more diverse, and more often living alone

  • The 65-and-over share rose from 12.9% to 16.7% while the under-18 share fell from 22.7% to 21.3%. The expansion ring is oldest at 20.0%.
  • The Hispanic or Latino share rose from 5.5% to 8.4%, and the White non-Hispanic share fell from 58.7% to 54.7%. Richmond city moved the other way: its White share rose from 39.7% to 41.4%.
  • 43.1% of Richmond city households are one person, against 61.4% married-couple families in the expansion ring. Region-wide, 29.6% of households are one person.
  • Real median household income in the Richmond MSA rose 9.6% to $86,679. Renters are far behind: 45.9% of renter households earn under $50,000, against 18.9% of owners.

Full detail: Demographics

The housing stock is single-family, older in the city, and built out in the suburbs

  • The three core localities held 404,918 housing units, up 13.3%; the expansion ring grew 18.4% from a much smaller base.
  • Single-family detached homes are 63% of the core’s stock and 88% of the ring’s. Only Richmond city is substantially multifamily (22% of units in buildings of 20 or more).
  • 38% of Richmond city’s housing predates 1950, against 2% in Chesterfield.
  • Vacancy is tight in the suburbs (3.8% in Chesterfield, 5.7% across the core) and highest in the southern localities (Sussex and Petersburg lead).
  • Permitting bottomed at 2,520 units in 2011, reached a series high of 10,414 in 2022, and was 9,091 in 2025. Multifamily outnumbered single-family permits in 3 of the last 4 years, and was 52% of 2025 units.

Prices rose faster than incomes; renters are no better off

  • After inflation, the Richmond MSA’s median home value rose 24.1% and median gross rent 11.3%, against 9.6% for median household income.
  • The typical home was worth 3.6 times the typical household income in the 2014 window and 4.1 times in 2024. Median rent equalled 20.0% of median income, from 19.7%.
  • Renter cost burden has not eased: 51.2% of renter households pay 30% or more of income on housing (51.4% in 2014), and 25.7% pay half or more. Owner burden fell from 26.1% to 20.0%.
  • Renter burden is now nearly identical across the core: 52.0% in Richmond city, 51.6% in Henrico, 52.2% in Chesterfield.
  • Ownership held near two-thirds (67.0%). Richmond city is the only core locality where renters outnumber owners (58,914 renter households to 45,407).

What to watch

Five things to look out for
  1. Growth leans on migration. With natural increase down to 1,451 a year, a slowdown in international or domestic moves would show up directly in population growth. International migration in 2025 was 50% of its 2024 peak.
  2. Home values are outrunning incomes. Real home values rose 24.1% against 9.6% for income, and the value-to-income ratio widened from 3.6 to 4.1. The value measure is every owner’s own estimate, so it understates the cost of buying today.
  3. Half of renters are cost-burdened, and the gap by income is wide. The share did not improve over the decade, and the typical renter household earns far less than the typical owner household.
  4. Production is volatile and its mix keeps changing. Permits swing between single-family and multifamily from year to year, and the expansion ring continues to build almost entirely detached homes (88% of its stock) even though 30% of the region’s households are one person.
  5. The outer suburbs are the oldest part of the region. The expansion ring has the highest 65-and-over share (20.0%) and, in aggregate, more deaths than births in 2020-2025 (-1,058), so its growth depends entirely on in-migration.
Reading the numbers
  • Stock and production are different series. Stock (ACS) counts units standing; production (Building Permits Survey) counts permits issued, which lead completions and can be cancelled.
  • Small geographies are noisy. The four expansion-ring localities and Ashland carry wide margins of error; the pages suppress cells with a coefficient of variation above 30.
  • Migration figures are modeled from administrative records and revised each vintage. The 2010-2019 and 2020-2025 series come from different vintages and are compared, not joined.
  • Dollar changes are inflation-adjusted with national CPI-U, so they measure change relative to national prices, not a local cost-of-living index.
  • See Data Notes for sources and definitions.
